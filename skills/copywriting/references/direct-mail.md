# Direct Mail

Use for physical-mail package strategy, recipient/list fit, formats, offers, response devices, and testing. Pair with [sales-letters.md](sales-letters.md) for letter craft and [awareness-levels.md](awareness-levels.md) for the opening strategy.

These are practitioner frameworks associated with Benson, Halbert, Stone and Jacobs, Kennedy, Bird, and Hatch. They are starting hypotheses, not guaranteed results. No source books are bundled. Verify quotations, numerical benchmarks, and specific historical attributions before presenting them as evidence.

## Recipient, offer, execution

The traditional “40–40–20” shorthand emphasizes recipient/list fit and offer before copy and format. It is not a measured allocation of success for the user's campaign.

Before polishing the piece, establish who receives it, why the offer fits, and what action makes economic sense. Existing customers, recent buyers, and cold prospects may respond differently; use actual campaign data rather than assuming a response multiplier.

Benson's testing mindset suggests useful hypotheses:

- Test a qualified list and a clear offer before small wording changes.
- Compare a personal letter presentation with an overt promotional format.
- Give a qualified reader enough information to decide; length alone is not a benefit.
- Test a lift note, reply device, or incentive when it has a clear role and the economics support it.
- Measure lead quality and downstream purchases as well as response count.

## Personal versus overtly promotional presentation

Halbert's “A-pile/B-pile” model distinguishes personal-looking correspondence from obvious advertising. Use it to choose a deliberate presentation, not to claim a guaranteed open rate.

A letter package may suit an offer needing explanation or trust. A postcard can communicate a simple local offer immediately. Personal presentation must accurately identify the sender and commercial purpose; never imply prior acquaintance, official status, an enclosed benefit, or a personal recommendation that does not exist.

A personal package can use a plain envelope, a readable letter from a real sender, a PS, and a simple reply route. A promotional piece can use a visible offer, clear headline, and prominent response device. Any teaser must be fulfilled by the contents.

## Format selection

| Format | Useful when | Tradeoff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Letter package | Explanation, considered purchases, fundraising, appointments | More production and postage; coordinate all pieces |
| Postcard | One simple offer, reminder, event, local service | Little privacy or room for nuance |
| Self-mailer | A concise promotion with visible offer | Must remain readable through folds and production constraints |
| Large envelope | More space or a substantial information package | Extra cost must be justified |
| Dimensional mail | A small, qualified list and a relevant physical object | Higher cost and handling; no assumed open-rate advantage |

Choose type size, contrast, and response routes for the actual audience and use context. Do not infer preferences from age alone. For older or low-vision readers, generous type and clear layout are useful starting points; validate legibility with the actual piece.

Envelope choices—teaser versus plain, stamp versus indicia, typed versus printed address—are test variables, not universal winners. Obtain current postal specifications when preparing final production files; do not invent a mailing-ready certification from a copy draft.

## Offer architecture

Specify:

1. What the reader receives: a product, service, consultation, information kit, or other actual offer.
2. The actual price, terms, and obligations.
3. Any genuine incentive and eligibility.
4. A real deadline or capacity limit, if one exists. An evergreen offer does not need fabricated urgency.
5. Actual risk-reduction terms, if available. Do not create a guarantee or imply no risk.

For a complex service, a useful first step may be an appointment or information request. Treat this as a strategic choice, not a rule that every letter must sell a consultation. A descriptive offer name can make the next step easier to understand; see [offers-pricing.md](offers-pricing.md).

## Reply path

Keep one primary conversion goal. Offer multiple response channels only when the sender can actually support them and the audience needs them.

- A reply card should restate the offer and request only necessary information.
- A phone instruction may name a real, available person when supplied.
- URLs and QR codes must resolve to the intended destination before production; placeholders remain clearly marked during drafting.
- Checkboxes or a short question can clarify intent; they do not guarantee a response lift.
- Explain what happens after the reader calls, replies, or submits a form.
- Keep the envelope, headline, body, PS, reply card, and destination consistent.

## Measurement and economics

Use a control when one exists. Compare meaningful changes—recipient/list segment, offer, format, then message—while accounting for assignment, seasonality, and follow-up. Keep attribution consistent across phone, mail, and web response.

Track response rate, qualified-lead rate, cost per lead, acquisition cost, and contribution after campaign costs. With stated assumptions:

`break-even response rate = total campaign cost / (pieces mailed × contribution per response)`

For lead generation, contribution per response depends on the lead-to-sale rate and contribution per sale. Do not substitute revenue for contribution or assume lifetime value without supporting retention/cost data.

Sample size depends on baseline response, detectable effect, power, uncertainty, and assignment—not a universal number of responses. Separate a promising directional result from a reliable decision. See [testing-methodology.md](testing-methodology.md).

## Follow-up and trust

Kennedy's message–market–media model asks whether the message fits both the recipients and the channel. A follow-up sequence may help; plan it only when in scope. Repetition must not manufacture an approaching deadline or imply the recipient responded when they did not.

For advice or regulated offers, use verified credentials and actual terms. Educational framing does not remove the need for accurate commercial disclosures. Identify any category-specific terms or production review still needed without inventing regulatory requirements or approvals.

## Preflight

- Recipient fit, offer, and primary action are clear.
- Sender identity and personalization are truthful.
- Envelope promise, letter, PS, and response device agree.
- Claims have evidence; missing phone numbers, URLs, and terms stay visible as placeholders.
- Deadlines, guarantees, and incentives appear only if real.
- Type, contrast, folds, and response routes fit the intended use.
- Cost assumptions, attribution, and any planned comparison are explicit.
- Production specifications and category requirements are checked where needed, or marked outstanding.
