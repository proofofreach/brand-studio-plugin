# Sales Letters

Use for long-form selling letters in print or digital form. For envelope, recipient/list, format, and response-device choices, use [direct-mail.md](direct-mail.md); for openings, [leads.md](leads.md); for structure, [body-copy.md](body-copy.md).

The frameworks below are associated with Collier, Caples, Sugarman, Halbert, Bird, Kennedy, and Bly. Treat them as craft heuristics, not universal laws or evidence of performance. Source books are not bundled. Verify exact quotations, attribution, and historical results before repeating them as facts.

## Collier: Start with the reader's situation

Open inside a relevant concern, desire, or decision the reader already has. Do not invent what the individual has experienced, what their account contains, or what they believe. Use audience evidence or frame the situation conditionally.

A useful sequence is opening → proposition → motive → proof → reason to act → close. Its purpose is to help a reader decide; omit or adapt elements that lack evidence or do not fit the offer. “Reason to act” need not mean a deadline.

## Caples: Specific appeals and testable openings

Self-interest, relevant news, and curiosity offer different headline angles. Test them against the audience and offer rather than declaring one a universal winner.

- Use specific facts and figures only when verified.
- Make the opening fulfill the headline's promise.
- Give qualified readers enough information; test length rather than assuming long copy wins.
- Make the requested action explicit.
- Treat examples from historical ads as examples, not results the user's product can claim.

## Sugarman: Momentum and buying context

Each element should make the next useful part easy to read: headline, subhead, image caption, opening, explanation, and close. Short openings and curiosity can help, but avoid empty cliffhangers and artificial withholding.

Match tone and presentation to the real offer. Explain objections where they arise. Admit a material limitation honestly when relevant; do not invent a flaw as a persuasion device. Stories, ownership imagery, familiarity, and hope should clarify the choice, not disguise costs or exaggerate outcomes. Do not reframe a preventive product as a cure unless that claim is accurate and supported.

## Halbert: A letter from a real sender

Write to one reader in a human voice. Use a supplied sender and truthful salutation; otherwise leave a clear placeholder. A personal style must not imply a relationship that does not exist.

Keep paragraphs readable, vary rhythm, and use emphasis sparingly. A PS is a useful second route through the offer: restate the main benefit and next step, plus a deadline or bonus only when real. Strong copy cannot rescue a badly matched audience and offer.

## Bird: Aim the letter at one next step

A helpful drafting order is offer → close → opening → middle. Every product fact should answer why it matters to the reader, without inventing a benefit the product cannot support.

Use one coherent voice and primary action. Read the letter as if explaining the offer face to face: remove corporate throat-clearing, confusing wordplay, and claims a responsible sender could not defend. Balance problem and solution for the situation; no fixed ratio is required.

## Kennedy: Know the reader, explain the offer

Use observed audience language and real objections. Do not present imagined fears or hidden motives as research findings.

Explain price and value with truthful comparisons. Cost-per-day or cost-of-inaction examples need valid arithmetic and explicit assumptions; they must not hide required payments or imply guaranteed savings.

Support two reading paths: the complete letter and a skim through headline, subheads, emphasis, and PS. Both should make the offer and action intelligible. After drafting, check strategy, then wording, then whether the claims and terms are fair to a person the sender respects.

## Flexible assembly template

1. Optional headline or offer summary that fits the presentation.
2. Honest salutation.
3. Reader-relevant opening.
4. Problem or opportunity with proportionate, supported stakes.
5. The approach, offer, and sender's relevant qualifications.
6. Actual price, terms, and obligations.
7. Verified proof near the claim it supports; a guarantee is an offer term, not proof of efficacy.
8. Clear next step and what happens afterward.
9. Real sender name/title, or a marked placeholder.
10. Optional PS that reinforces the offer and action.

Use readable type, adequate contrast, short paragraphs, and natural section/page breaks. Avoid forcing a mid-sentence page turn or a fixed opening word count at the expense of comprehension. Design for the actual format and audience.

## Review

- The opening fits the reader without pretending to know private facts.
- The headline promise is fulfilled.
- Features connect to supportable benefits.
- Claims, testimonials, numbers, and credentials have evidence.
- Objections and material terms are clear.
- The offer stands without fabricated deadlines or guarantees.
- The full and skim reading paths agree.
- The action and next steps are concrete and consistent with the reply device.
- The sender and personalization are authentic.
- The PS, if used, introduces no unsupported promise.
